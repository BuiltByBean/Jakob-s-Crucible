## ***THE CENTRAL TENSION***

My understanding of biblical faith
has evolved substantially as I’ve progressed
beyond the simple children's church lessons
to ask the obvious questions that arise
from a close examination of the Scriptures
pertaining to the nature of faith.

That’s not to complain
about the way I was taught as a child,
but merely to point out
that a childish understanding of faith
is not enough to answer the kinds of questions
that a more mature mind may consider,
such as when trying to reconcile
human will and responsibility
with the doctrine of grace-enabled
and God-dependent faith.

In case you feel that by saying that
I should be rebuked along with the disciples
for rejecting child-like faith,
let me first remind you that in Matthew 18:4,
Jesus is responding to a question that probably stemmed from their sinful human pride.

What He said was,
“Whoever humbles himself like this child
is the greatest in the kingdom of Heaven.”

I believe that the key word in that verse
reveals to us the value
that He is teaching in this passage,
which may help to clarify
Luke 18:17 and Mark 10:15 as well.

How do we receive the gift of faith,
the blood of Christ, and the Kingdom of Heaven?

Humbly, because we have done nothing to earn it.

A newborn has done nothing
to earn its mother’s love by the time of birth.

In fact, the baby has spent months
making her miserable to some degree
and adding nothing tangibly helpful
to the mother’s daily life.

I don’t love my children
because they have somehow earned it.

As parents, we love our children
because God loves his children,
and He **designed** us to love our children.

So, barely past the introduction,
the theology of Faith has already taught us something about God’s fatherly nature
and our relationship to Him as children to a parent:

He offers out of love what we could not earn
yet so desperately require
for our very survival into eternity.

Furthermore, God also tells us in Proverbs c. 22 v. 6
to train up a child in the way they should go,
and in Mark c. 12 v. 30 to love God with all our strength, soul, heart, and **mind**.

I can try to support a variety of opinions
from any one of these verses,
but the synthesis of Scripture
is that I receive the Gospel
with child-like humility and dependence
and reach to grasp its meaning and implications
so that I am not only loving God
emotionally or religiously
but with my mind also
through the pursuit of understanding and wisdom.

Now,
as I step off of my first soapbox of this episode,
let us refocus on the task at hand.

For the purpose of this episode,
I’m going to define the central tension
through the following questions:

Does Scripture teach that faith itself is a gift,
and if so,
why and how does it teach us
to believe and receive faith?

Often when I’ve heard faith discussed
in a sermon or conversation,
the emphasis is on our need to **be** faithful.

While I don’t think that’s necessarily wrong,
it does allow the inference of some people
that faith is dependent solely on us;
as though it’s our side of the bargain,
the thing that **we** bring into our relationship with God.

In contrast, when reading carefully crafted
doctrinal statements of faith,
what I have also often seen
is the emphasis instead being on the gifting of grace and our dependence on God for it.

In fact,
for many professing the Christian faith,
this is not only a matter of emphasis
but a genuine difference in doctrine
with far-reaching implications
for other aspects of theology such as salvation
and the unchanging attributes of God.

While there are innumerable nuanced perspectives that could be considered,
I believe that the three most common
understandings of faith within Christianity
are as follows:

faith as man’s contribution
faith as a grace-enabled response
and faith as the inevitable result of irresistible grace.

I am going to propose the following as a summary
of my conclusion after studying Scripture
and considering my own
personal experience with God.

Without God’s initiative to reach out to us,
we would not be able to acquire faith independently, and through God’s gracious initiative to create us
and to provide the faculties and call necessary
for the opportunity and ability
to perceive and respond to his self-revelation,
He enables us to participate in relationship with Him through faith.

That means that faith is impossible to acquire
apart from God’s grace,
but He still holds each individual accountable
for their response to the Gospel
through their God-given gift of free will.

Furthermore,
knowing that faith is given in the context
of God's foreknowledge of our response,
**I** consider the nature of faith as a gift
to be further revelation of His mercy
**rather** than evidence for determinism
because foreknowledge by itself
does not require the conclusion that God coerces
or effectively forces our decision.

While He could rightfully do that,
acknowledging God’s foreknowledge
only really **requires** the conclusion
that He has always known what choice will be made even before we have the chance to make that choice.

This would lend support to a theory that some hold to that his desire to save people from suffering
could be a potential motive
for what the Bible describes
as God hardening the hearts
of those who ultimately reject the Faith
since greater revelation imparts greater responsibility,
which in turn requires greater consequence
for rebellion.

I won’t be building a case for that in this discussion.

Although, for now,
I consider it reasonable speculation
because it seems plausible to infer from the text,
but it is also not explicitly stated
and does not seem to me as of yet obviously implied
as a comprehensive answer
to the question regarding hardened hearts.

Returning to the topic of Faith itself,
I also want to point out
before I present the primary evidence
that the tension between the gifting
and command to receive is not due to contradiction, but I think, most likely,
it is due to the difficulty that some people have
with either accepting or understanding
that they are responsible for their choice
to receive and participate with that gift.

If all of that seems at first overly convoluted,
I think the examination of relevant Scriptures
one at a time followed by an examination
of the metanarrative of the Bible
will make it clear enough by the end of this study.

## ***NOT BLIND BELIEF, BUT APPROPRIATE RESPONSE***

As we examine Scriptures
pertaining to the nature of Faith,
I want to begin with those that differentiate
biblical Faith from what I refer to as blind belief.

The reason being
that one who has blind belief without good reason
can also be led astray
to blind disbelief without good reason.

Some people use the term “blind faith”
to describe this same concept,
but biblical Faith is by definition not blind,
so instead I prefer the term “blind belief”
which doesn’t necessarily imply evidence or reasoning.

A belief can be reasoned or whimsical,
whereas genuine Faith requires genuine reason.

Faith does not require walking through life in darkness imposed by religious blindfolding.

It is letting go of the blindfold of the enemy
that keeps us in darkness
so that we can walk in the light of God’s glory.

In this way,
the Faith that we are called to reflects the mind
of a God who is not threatened
by honest examination, evidence, or reasoning, because He is Himself the source of truth.

General revelation in creation
provides enough evidence to leave us with no excuse
to disbelieve the reality of our Creator,
special revelation through the Scriptures
and incarnation of Christ has brought us the Gospel, and the work of God in every believer’s spirit
is the final nail in the coffin
of our once-destined death.

Except that while the grave is destined to hold
the old nature of sin and death forever,
it has no hold on the reborn spirit of the Church
that is made new by the Holy Spirit!

Faith does not manufacture truth.
Faith receives truth
from the God who originates and reveals it.

Listen to what Scripture has to say about this,
beginning with Romans c. 1 v. 18-21.

For the wrath of God is revealed from Heaven
against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth.

19 For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them.

20 For his invisible attributes,
namely, his eternal power and divine nature,
have been clearly perceived,
ever since the creation of the world,
in the things that have been made.

So they are without excuse.

21 For although they knew God,
they did not honor Him as God or give thanks to Him, but they became futile in their thinking,
and their foolish hearts were darkened.

End quote.

In Scripture, we find that no one has an excuse.

There is a general revelation of, at the bare minimum, the power and divinity of God
through the witness of creation.

It is futile and foolish thinking
that prevents people from recognizing God in creation, even without hearing the Gospel of Christ.

The foolishness is in the rejection of reality.

The futility is in the inability
to change that reality through disbelief.

The Creator doesn’t stop existing
just because the creation pretends that He isn’t real.

Let’s next consider Romans c. 2 v. 12-16.

For all who have sinned without the law
will also perish without the law,
and all who have sinned under the law
will be judged by the law.

13 For it is not the hearers of the law
who are righteous before God,
but the doers of the law who will be justified.

14 For when Gentiles, who do not have the law,
by nature do what the law requires,
they are a law to themselves,
even though they do not have the law.

15 They show that the work of the law
is written on their hearts,
while their conscience also bears witness,
and their conflicting thoughts accuse
or even excuse them 16 on that day when,
according to my Gospel,
God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus.

End quote.

In this letter to the Romans,
God reveals that not only do we have
the external witness of creation
to direct our attention to God,
but we also have his law written on our hearts,
which we experience through what is called
the conscience.

The obvious evidence is in the substantial overlap
of moral laws across the ages
and cultures of the world.

A man’s conscience must be dulled
by his own self-deception
to believe that something such as murder is acceptable because God granted us the law
written into our beings to afford us a guide
to show us how to combat our sin nature.

By understanding this,
we can also understand that the truth of God’s nature and presence is not hidden by the lack of evidence.

Rather,
human perception is obscured by the darkness of sin.

Next, we will consider Romans c. 10 v. 14–17.

How then will they call on Him
in whom they have not believed?

And how are they to believe in Him
of whom they have never heard?

And how are they to hear without someone preaching?

15 And how are they to preach unless they are sent?

As it is written,
“How beautiful are the feet of those
who preach the good news!”

16 But they have not all obeyed the gospel.

For Isaiah says,
“Lord, who has believed what he has heard from us?”

17 So faith comes from hearing,
and hearing through the word of Christ.

End quote.

This passage teaches us that Faith in Christ comes from hearing the Gospel of Christ.

Without some form of exposure to the Gospel of Christ, you would not know who Christ is
and have the opportunity to have Faith in Him.

From this we can also know
that Faith is not a blind self-generated belief,
but a response to the revelation of God.

As with the divine attributes
apparent from general revelation,
reading the Bible and listening to preachers
are not the only ways to receive revelation
of the Gospel of Christ either.

Paul knew of the Gospel.
Although I can’t define the limits of what he knew
prior to his conversion,
I do know what Acts c. 9 tells us,
which is that he was still breathing threats of murder against the followers of Jesus Christ
right up until the moment
that Jesus revealed Himself personally to Paul
on the road to Damascus.

I’ve heard of numerous other stories
of people having dreams, visions,
or other types of encounters with God since then; some long ago and some in the modern day.

My point is that Romans c. 10 is not saying
that people who aren’t evangelized by conventional natural methods are simply out of luck.

That still doesn’t remove the urgency for evangelism, because even though God **can**
evangelize supernaturally,
He also gave us the Great Commission in Matthew 28; commanding that we go out and make disciples.

One truth that we must remember
is that our commission doesn’t shift the responsibility for saving people from God to us.

What it does do
is provide yet more miraculous evidence for God
in the form of sinners transformed by the Holy Spirit
proclaiming their death and resurrection in Christ!

Next, consider with me Hebrews c. 2 v. 2-4.

For since the message declared by angels
proved to be reliable,
and every transgression or disobedience
received a just retribution,
3 how shall we escape
if we neglect such a great salvation?

It was declared at first by the Lord,
and it was attested to us by those who heard,
4 while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will.

End quote.

Why did the writer of Hebrews imply
that there is a great offer of salvation
that they can’t neglect?

Because the message declared by the angels
**proved to be reliable**.

Not, because they had a hunch, or a gut feeling,
or they felt at peace about it all,
but because it was proven to them.

God the Son brought the Gospel,
God the Father testified to the legitimacy of the Son through the miracles culminating
with the resurrection,
and God the Spirit confirms the fruit of salvation through spiritual gifts.

The first generation of Jesus’s disciples
did not have a blind faith.

The Gospel of John tells us in c. 20 v. 11-14
that Mary Magdalene was weeping outside of the tomb when Jesus first appeared to her.

He asked why she was weeping.
In response, she claimed that her Lord’s body
had been stolen from the tomb!

Despite the fact that Jesus had predicted his death **and** resurrection to his disciples
prior to the crucifixion,
Mary Magdalene did not presume
that Jesus had risen from the dead at that time; probably because, like most people,
she understood that the dead aren’t known
for walking out of their tombs.

He had to say her name and draw her full attention
to Himself for her to recognize Him.

Yet, she was so thoroughly convinced
from a close inspection of Him
that she immediately obeyed his command
to go to the other disciples
and proclaim the resurrection!

The related passage in Luke c. 24 notes in v. 11
that after Mary and the other women with her proclaimed the resurrection to the apostles,
they considered it no more than idle chatter!

This is the kind of embarrassing detail
that you wouldn’t add to your story
if you were giving a false witness
or trying to start a baseless religion.

The apostles, of whom several later went on
to die a martyr’s death for the Gospel of Christ,
did not believe the Gospel when they first heard it.

Even when Jesus appeared before them later in v. 36
and spoke to them face-to-face,
the text informs us that they initially thought
they saw a spirit.

To their credit,
John c. 20 v. 19 notes that the doors were locked when Jesus appeared before them,
seeming to imply that Jesus entered the room miraculously without opening the door.

In their shoes
I probably would have thought He were a spirit as well, or that I was out of my mind,
which is probably why Jesus appeared
at least 11 times before his ascension
to people individually, in small groups,
and also to over 500 people at once.

There’s no logical foundation
for rationalizing that historical account as hundreds
of people all sharing the same hallucination
of the same risen Savior,
especially since they were initially convinced
that He was dead and were evidently more prone
to believe in the appearance of a ghost
than a bodily resurrection.

John c. 20 v. 24-28 tells us
that Thomas refused to believe in the Gospel
until 8 days after people had begun to report
the resurrection when Jesus again appeared suddenly within a locked room and told Thomas
to place his fingers in the scars of his Savior.

The apostles did not have a blind faith.

They responded, finally, and appropriately,
when their resurrected Lord appeared before them
and proved Himself to them, personally.

Despite their initial disbelief,
He still pursued them
to the depths of their confusion and despair
to change everything.

Is that not also
the testimony of most Christians today?

Nearly every time that I have heard someone explain **why** they believe Christ is Lord,
it boils down to the fact that they genuinely believe that God has revealed Himself to them
and renewed their spirit,
changing their very identity.

We do not have a blind faith.

Our Faith is an appropriate response
to the revelation of God in our lives.

You will not believe in God because I tell you to.

You will believe in God
because He is revealed in creation
and active in your life.

## ***GRACE — WHAT & WHEREFORE ART THOU?***

Having established that Faith is not blind,
we now need to confirm why revelation alone
is not enough.

Therefore,
the next step in this theology of Faith
requires us to understand the necessity of grace
and its function in the process of redemption
for mankind.

This requires much more than simply agreeing
that God covers our sins with his grace,
because while that is enough for children’s church
or elevator-speech evangelism,
it doesn’t explain what grace is and why it’s necessary.

So,
let’s consider the shape of our need
and how God’s grace immaculately fills
that desperate need.

To state it simply,
for mankind,
the ministry of grace is necessary for life
because the ministry of law provides us only death.

Consider 2 Corinthians c. 3 v. 4-6.

Such is the confidence that we have through Christ toward God.

5 Not that we are sufficient in ourselves
to claim anything as coming from us,
but our sufficiency is from God,
6 who has made us sufficient
to be ministers of a new covenant,
not of the letter but of the Spirit.

For the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.

End quote.

The Scripture’s meaning here is clear:
If all that we had was the law,
we would remain dead in our sins —
destined for an eternity separated
from our just Creator.

But why is it that the law requires our death?

Because as is plainly stated in Romans 6:23,
“the wages of sin is death.”

Some might wonder who that applies to.

Who has sinned and earned death?

The answer is quite simple —
all who fall short of the glory of God —
so, who has fallen short of the glory of God?

The answer, yet again, is quite simple
and quite unfortunate:
all have fallen short of the glory of God.

Consider Romans c. 3 v. 19-31.

Now we know that whatever the law says
it speaks to those who are under the law,
so that every mouth may be stopped,
and the whole world may be held accountable to God.

20 For by works of the law
no human being will be justified in his sight,
since through the law comes knowledge of sin.

21 But now the righteousness of God
has been manifested apart from the law,
although the Law and the Prophets
bear witness to it —
22 the righteousness of God
through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe.

For there is no distinction:
23 for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, 24 and are justified by his grace as a gift,
through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,
25 whom God put forward
as a propitiation by his blood,
to be received by faith.

This was to show God’s righteousness, 
because in his divine forbearance
He had passed over former sins.

26 It was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that He might be just
and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.

27 Then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded.

By what kind of law? By a law of works?

No, but by the law of faith.

28 For we hold that one is justified by faith
apart from works of the law.

29 Or is God the God of Jews only?
Is He not the God of Gentiles also?

Yes, of Gentiles also, 30 since God is one —
who will justify the circumcised by faith
and the uncircumcised through faith.

31 Do we then overthrow the law by this faith?
By no means!

On the contrary, we uphold the law.

End quote.

So,
because all have sinned and fallen short
of the standard of righteousness,
we are dependent on the redemptive love of God
for salvation through Faith in Jesus Christ
by the sacrifice of his blood to pay for our sin.

In this way,
God has satisfied his law’s requirement
of righteousness and justice through Jesus.

In other words,
grace does not mean that God ignores justice,
but that He fulfills it in Himself for the sake of sinners.

He provides the means for Faith in Christ
on our behalf and calls us to join in this faith —
which is offered to us by grace
because we cannot earn it.

This idea comes straight from the writings of Paul
in Romans c. 11 v. 6.

If it is by grace,
it is no longer on the basis of works;
otherwise, grace would no longer be grace.

End quote.

Anyone who earns their way into Heaven
by upholding the perfect standard of righteousness doesn’t need grace.

Grace is by definition something that is not earned, and according to Scripture
our Faith for salvation is dependent on grace.

Thus, the act of believing,
which we do in obedient response to God’s call,
is not a work.

It is merely our humble open-handed response
to accept the gift of salvation.

For faith to be a work,
it would have to earn salvation,
but consider this:

you don’t treat gifts that way
when given by someone other than God,
do you?

If someone gives you a birthday gift,
do you negate the gifting and earn that gift
by willingly receiving it?

No, of course not.

Furthermore,
any good work that we do accomplish
is too late to save us,
because from the earliest days
that we can be held accountable for,
we all have sinned.

God’s standard is perfection —
not being perfect for a moment,
but endless, eternal perfection.

From the first sin of your life,
you earned separation from God
according to his justice as taught through the law,
so you should repent and believe earnestly
in the Gospel of Christ —
the gift of grace that allows you to participate
in a redeemed relationship with God —
because the Christ is the only man
to ever live a sinless life
by perfectly upholding the standard of righteousness.

It is because He upheld the law
that He was able to suffer on our behalf
the consequences of sin and offer to us saving grace.

Humanity’s deepest problem has never been
lacking information as to how to obtain salvation.

Humanity’s deepest problem is the spiritual corruption of the sinful nature of the flesh.

We are easily inclined to suppress the light of truth
to preserve the ignorance afforded
by the darkness that we cling to.

This leads to moral failure,
which further incentivizes us to cling to the dark rather than risk the discomfort of exposure
and disinfection in the light.

This destructive cycle produces
exactly one fruit in our lives —
death, as we already know from Romans 6:23.

In John c. 6 Jesus talks about the bread of life.

He reminds the Jews that after the exodus from Egypt, the Israelites in the wilderness
ate the manna from Heaven,
but they all still died
at the eventual end of their mortality.

Christians eat the bread of life
and are sustained by it for eternity.

This parallels the reality of human works
failing to save us from the death required by the law, whereas the gift of Faith
is the means by which God provides
for completed salvation in us.

This passage teaches us many things,
one of which is the dependence of the Christian Faith on God’s grace.

In the interest of understanding the context
that informs the interpretive work that comes next,
I’m going to read John c. 6 v. 24-65.

Now that we’ve examined the entire scene,
let’s take a closer look at a few theological statements that Jesus made through this passage,
starting with v. 27-29.

This exchange is composed of a theological statement, followed by a question,
followed by a clarifying statement
regarding the original statement.

First, in v. 27, Jesus tells the people
that instead of working for food that perishes,
they should work for the food that sustains eternal life.

In v. 28, the people ask Jesus to clarify
what work is considered the work of God
which leads to eternal life.

Finally, in v. 29, Jesus reveals that “work”
in his metaphor is not meritorious labor,
but believing in the Gospel
that Jesus is bringing to them.

It’s important to follow the flow of ideas
throughout the whole conversation
rather than just interpreting one verse at a time,
because if you read v. 29 out of context
then you could adopt
one of a couple easy misinterpretations.

Due to the translated wording “the work of God”, which was the same
in all 4 common translations that I checked,
you might think that Jesus is saying
that God is the one doing the work of believing.

Another possible
and more serious error, I think,
would be to interpret Jesus to be teaching
that belief in Him is a meritorious work,
meaning that it actually contributes to you meriting
or earning grace from God for salvation.

Jesus is not using the word for work in this discussion
the way that theologians typically do
in soteriological discussions —
those pertaining to the topic of salvation.

You would have a hard time justifying
that interpretation of John 6:29
in light of all of the other Scriptures
that describe grace as a gift
that we cannot earn for ourselves,
but as earned entirely by the salvific work
of Jesus through the cross.

Jesus is simply being consistent with the
metaphorical language that He had been using
from the start of that discussion when He told
the people not to work for food that perishes,
but for that which leads to eternal life.

The point was not that belief is a work,
but that they should stop focusing their labor
on things that would not be beneficial for eternity.

Furthermore, if you examine the Greek in v. 29,
you’ll find that it uses a form
of the same Greek word for ‘work’
as is used in 1 Corinthians 15:58,
which says the following:

My beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable,
always abounding in the work of the Lord,
knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain.

End quote.

How could the work of the Lord refer
to something God Himself is doing
rather than godly work that we are doing
when in the very same breath it says
to not let “your labor” be in vain?

That’s obviously not referring to God’s labor,
but our own human labor.

The point is not that we work for our salvation,
but that because of our salvation
we should do good works.

Acquiring salvation isn’t a business-like transaction where we simply and justly acquire something
for a payment of our own means.

You cannot be genuinely indwelt
by the Holy Spirit of God
that brings the fruits discussed in Galatians 5
and converts doubters to martyrs
without being transformed into a new being
that is more inclined to godliness.

That increased inclination to godliness
that the Spirit of God develops in us
as we experience ongoing sanctification
to become more like God
should result in us doing more godly works,
not to merit salvation for the sake of self-preservation,
but for the sake of being obedient to God
and becoming more like God
so that you can bring glory to his name
and serve as salt and light in this cursed world!

Doing good works may benefit you,
but that should not be your focus.

Your focus should be on blessing God and others.

That is a sign of the eternal gift of the Spirit,
which is love.

I have more to discuss regarding that
in a later section of this episode
in which we’ll consider the fruitfulness of Faith.

For now,
the primary point is that belief or faith in the Gospel
is not a meritorious work that earns salvation.

Let’s return to John c. 6
to consider carefully Jesus’s statement in v. 44.

He says,
“no one can come to Me
unless the Father who sent Me draws him”.

This is the verse in this passage
that I think is most likely to be used
by a Reformed or Calvinist theologian to support
a doctrine of deterministic irresistible grace.

That doctrine asserts that because humans
are so completely spiritually depraved
God must save us by effectively calling those
who are predestined
according to his unconditional election
which I would argue necessarily implies
that it **functionally** disregards our individual wills
since it requires grace to either
ignore or bypass our wills
or forcibly or effectually change our wills
such that it removes the genuine possibility
of ultimately rejecting the Gospel.

This also seems to necessarily imply
that those who are destined for Hell
have effectively been prevented from ever having
a genuine opportunity to receive salvation
since they are not provided the irresistible grace
that is necessary to overcome total depravity.

In essence, the punch line is this:

If the total depravity of humanity means that no one can receive salvation without irresistible grace,
and God withholds that necessary
irresistible grace from the non-elect,
then the non-elect never have a genuine possibility
of receiving salvation.

One such as myself
who does not conform to Reformed theology
and does not affirm this doctrine of irresistible grace might argue that such a doctrine **effectively** removes
our personal responsibility for the reception of Faith
since we are apparently not able to make a decision one way or the other of our own wills.

Genuine responsibility requires genuine choice,
which seems functionally removed
under the doctrine of irresistible grace.

Of course,
a Reformed theologian
will not likely appreciate the way
that I’ve described the irresistible grace doctrine
because my description emphasizes what I understand to be an obviously unbiblical implication which seems necessarily derivative of that doctrine.

I wouldn’t break fellowship with someone
over this disagreement,
but I still consider it a serious doctrinal disagreement that can impact the way that we represent God
to the world as his image bearers,
as well as our broader theological beliefs
such as the nature of our relationship with God
and the urgent purpose of evangelism.

That said,
let’s consider the key question
that leads us to the doctrine of Faith
that is truly taught in John 6:44.

That question is this:
What does Jesus mean when He says
that the Father draws people to Him?

Does the draw of the Father coerce
or force our compliance,
or does it still allow and require the response
of our individual wills to accept the call
that leads to belief and Faith?

A Reformed or Calvinist theologian may propose
the argument that the second half of v. 44,
where Jesus says,
“and I will raise him up on the last day”,
indicates that everyone drawn by the Father
**must** be saved.

The problem with this claim
is that it requires the assumption
that Jesus is defining every person whom God
ever draws rather than describing just those
who actually respond to Him.

The one who comes to Jesus in Faith
will certainly be raised,
but this does not prove
that God draws to Himself in such a way
as to prevent the possibility of resistance —
especially when John c. 12 v. 32 says
that Christ will draw all people to Himself
and Scripture plainly teaches
that not all people will be saved.

It’s easy enough to simply contest isolated verses
that seem to imply an irresistible election
by pointing to those that seem to require
a free will choice,
but first,
I want to show the interpretive extent and limitation
of the Greek in this passage
and a related verse that comes shortly thereafter.

The reason that I think this can be helpful
is that while having English translations is great
for people who can’t read Greek,
the syntax of a translation is not necessarily inspired in the same way that the original writing
of the Scripture was inspired by the Holy Spirit.

I say that because I know
that people sometimes hinge entire doctrines
on the wording of a particular translation
of a particular verse that doesn’t always fit very well with the original text.

This is why I try to remember
to check multiple translations
and occasionally perform word studies
for key words that could change the development
of entire doctrines.

So, let’s consider a little Greek
and Jesus’s use of the word translated “draw”
in multiple contexts.

John 12:32 records Jesus saying
that He “will draw all people to” Himself.

In this case, Strong’s definition for **helko**,
the Greek word translated to “draw” in this text,
indicates that it can be used
in a literal **or** figurative sense.

I would also like to note
that the Greek uses the term **pantas**, a form of **pas** rendered in this text as “all people”,
which, like the term for “draw”,
is also not a special term that can decide this issue
by its definition alone.

In short, from researching these terms,
I have found no indication
that the definitions of these terms alone
require narrowing the scope
from including all individuals,
whether elect or not.

In both cases,
according to the normal semantic range,
there is room for interpretation.

That being true,
it’s clear that we must look beyond
these isolated terms to their greater context
to interpret the intended meaning of this Scripture.

To that end,
consider that for the word “draw”
to indicate an irresistible summons,
one of two things must be true.

Either all people who have ever lived must be saved, which would be universalism,
or the “all people” in this text
must only refer to all who will receive Christ.

First, I will note that universalism directly contradicts the passages of Scripture that refer to those
who will not be allowed into the Kingdom of God.

Second, I will also note that the narrow application
of this statement to Christians or the elect exclusively
is not apparent in the text of John.

The best evidence that I have found
for supporting that interpretation says
that because Jesus was speaking to Jews at the time,
and Greeks were mentioned earlier in the text,
perhaps He meant to explain
that Gentiles would be included in the drawing
along with Jews.

That proposition might allow one
to still affirm the possibility of irresistible grace,
but only **if** the context requires
such a narrowed reading,
and the weight of that “if” seems concerning to me
since the purpose of this speculation
is to reconcile a doctrine of grace
with biblical principles that I would say
preclude any definition of grace
that functionally excludes the genuine possibility
of individual choice to receive the gift of Faith.

A point of inclusion for Gentiles and Jews
together in the Faith can be true without proving
that “all people” means only “all who receive Christ”.

So, consider this:

Do you believe that all people who have ever lived
will be ultimately saved unto eternity,
as universalism affirms?

If so, who then are the goats
whom Jesus will separate from the sheep
to be sent to the eternal fire according to his parable
in Matthew c. 25 v. 31-46;
they to whom Jesus will say,
“I was hungry and you gave me no food”?

Who then are the invited guests found unworthy
in the parable of the wedding feast
between Christ and his Church
in Matthew c. 22 v. 1-14?

My point regarding the text of John c. 12
is that it seems to plainly refer to literally all people with no apparent qualifier to narrow the scope.

This leaves me with the conclusion
that the text genuinely says what it appears to say: that God will draw everyone to Himself.

My next question is,
does this fit with the character of God
as revealed throughout Scripture?

Indeed, it fits quite well,
as is evidenced through numerous other passages such as 1 Timothy 2:4 and 2 Peter 3:9.

1 Timothy 2:4 says that “God our Savior…
desires all people to be saved
and to come to the knowledge of the truth.”

2 Peter 3:9 says that “the Lord… is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish,
but that all should reach repentance.”

As a minor point of critique,

I find it interesting that some seem to suggest
the terms “draw” and “grant repentance” in the texts
are proof of unconditional election,

yet it seems to me that this case is no more convincing than if one were to say
that 2 Peter must imply human sufficiency
since it reveals God’s desire
that “all **reach** repentance”.

If drawing and granting must imply human passivity by definition **or** connotation,
then perhaps one could also say
that to “**reach**”
conversely must imply only human initiative.

To avoid any confusion regarding this point,
that is not my actual argument.

I’m only suggesting
that to argue from the position
that God’s drawing and granting
must necessarily imply
only God’s action,

**which** **therefore** excludes
functionally meaningful individual agency
to accept or reject the Gospel,

is just as faulty as arguing from the position
that human reaching must necessarily imply
only human action.

**which therefore** excludes the necessity
of God’s initiation.

Thus, yet again,
I must insist that we cannot hinge
our understanding of this doctrine
on isolated terms,
but on the synthesis of all Scripture.

That being said,
if God desires for all to be saved,
draws all to Himself,
and yet not all are saved,
then the idea of the chosen being drawn to God
cannot be flattened and distorted
into irresistible coercion.

At this point,
insisting on another interpretation of John 6
seems less likely to me
to be a result of responsible exegesis,
drawing the natural meaning of the text,
and more likely eisegesis,
reading an unnatural interpretation into the text, motivated by the need to satisfy a theological agenda that must interpret v. 44 as irresistible grace
that **effectively** forces faith upon us
regardless of our wills.

I know that those holding to truly irresistible grace probably wouldn’t like that description,
but I haven’t been able to find or figure
a better explanation that doesn’t blatantly contradict other plain statements of Scripture
in all of my time studying this issue,
so I think my description is fair.

If you interpret Jesus in John 6 to mean that everyone whom God will draw must respond with Faith,
then I suggest that you should also interpret
the same connotation for “draw” in c. 12
to be consistent,
as there is no apparent indication in the text
to convey a different meaning.

Let’s consider what Jesus says in v. 63-65.

‘ “It is the Spirit who gives life;
the flesh is no help at all.

The words that I have spoken to you
are spirit and life.

64 But there are some of you who do not believe.”

65 And He said,
“This is why I told you that no one can come to Me unless it is granted him by the Father.” ’

End quote.

With that last statement in v. 65,
Jesus reveals the explanation
for why no one can come to Him
without the Father granting it.

The theological basis came just prior in v. 63:
“the Spirit gives life, the flesh is no help at all.”

This shouldn’t be surprising at this point,
because we’ve already established
that only the Spirit of God is the source of life
and the one who renews our spirits.

Furthermore,
only God can reveal Himself according to his will.

If He did not desire and act to reveal Himself,
then we would have no ability to seek and find Him.

That’s the contrast between the inability of the flesh and the necessity of God
to reach out with the offer of grace.

This is why no one can come to Jesus
without the Father granting it:
without the call of God to direct us
we are like the blind searching for a light.

More than merely lost,
we are genuinely incapable
of overcoming the corrupted flesh without God’s grace.

As with physical blindness,
the blind in this simile
can be both totally dependent on a helper
and yet also responsible for accepting or rejecting
the necessary offer of guidance from a helper.

Regarding this discussion of grace and Faith,
the conclusion that I’m left with
is that God enables our Faith
through his gracious call to believe in Jesus.

This is where grace fits in.

While it is according to God’s justice
that He condemns evil,
it is according to his grace
that He also offers the gift of Faith
through the call to believe and respond
to the Gospel of Christ.

If He did not freely offer it,
we could not choose to receive it.

Because of this,
Faith is a gift, fully dependent on God,
but only released from his hand
into our lives to be experienced
after our willing reception that requires,
as God decrees through Scripture,
that we repent and believe.

This is not because God **couldn’t** force it upon us,
but because it is his expressed will
that we participate with Him
in a genuinely loving relationship.

As we know from Scripture’s handling of godly love,
the difference between genuine love
and robotic or coerced servitude
is the genuine ability and option
to receive Him, or reject Him.

This is why,
despite Faith being a gift dependent on God’s grace,
we are still each held personally accountable
for our response to receive it.

## ***THE EMPHASIS ON RECEPTION***

So,
now that we understand
that Faith which leads to salvation is a gift,
we can move on to the next most obvious question regarding the nature of Faith.

I don’t think this is a particularly perplexing question,
but its mere presence is enough
for some people to claim
that there’s a contradiction between the prior idea
and what I’ll present
as the biblical answer to that question.

Furthermore,
how this question is answered
is one of the most notable differences
between the original primary branches
of the Protestant tradition
that broke away from Catholicism in the 16th century with the intent to return to the apostolic roots
and re-align the Church with Scripture first.

In my own words,
the question that I’m referring to is this:

If Faith is a gift freely given,
how do we interpret
the frequent command throughout Scripture
to believe, to have faith, to be faithful?

I’ve spent a lot of time thus far in this episode emphasizing the gifted nature of Faith,
but I can easily show a larger sample size of verses commanding people to be faithful in a way
that gives some people the impression
that it’s something that we provide independently
from within ourselves.

That said,
you may have heard that context is king
when it comes to interpreting Scripture.

Regarding this discussion at the least,
I think that doesn’t quite
sufficiently express the necessary caution,
because when we are trying to draw implications
for a whole theological system from single verses,
we need much more than just the **immediate** context of that sentence, paragraph, or even chapter.

We need to consider the entire book
and how that book fits within the greater context
of Scripture as a whole.

We need to consider the purpose and implications
of that isolated text in the grand metanarrative,
or over-arching story,
that God has authored through creation.

First and foremost, we must acknowledge
that **Scripture interprets Scripture**,
and the writers of Scripture were **not**
a collection of isolated ancient imbeciles
making up a religion as they went along.

They were spiritually inspired disciples
of the one true all-knowing God.

What that means for us in this circumstance
is that when two small snippets of Scripture
seem like they **could** contradict each other,
or at least don’t seem to be painting
quite the same picture,
we need to expand our perspective
and consider the greater framework of our great God
to understand how they fit together.

There are no contradictions
in the inspired truth claims of Scripture.

There are **recordings** of people
whose **words** contradict claims of Scripture,
but those are descriptive rather than prescriptive:
records of **others’** errors discussed
within the **inerrant** Word of God.

If describing faith as a gift and as something
that we are responsible for taking hold of
still seems contradictory to you,
then carefully considering both of these perspectives
as they are offered throughout Scripture
and how they can complement each other
should reveal the missing piece.

Let’s do that by considering now
the emphasis of Scripture on our reception
and personal responsibility,
beginning with the beautifully grace-revealing analogy in John c. 12 v. 35-36.

In that passage,
Jesus is recorded saying this to the people:

“The light is among you for a little while longer.

Walk while you have the light,
lest darkness overtake you.

The one who walks in the darkness
does not know where he is going.

**36** While you have the light, believe in the light,
that you may become sons of light.”

End quote.

In this analogy,
Jesus explains to us that God gives light,
but people are also commanded to respond to it.

How can people be responsible for belief
if it is enabled by grace?

By the same means
that we are responsible with the choices that we make based on what we **can** see
even though we need an external source of light
in order to see.

The very physiological mechanism of sight
teaches us something profound about God!

Our eyes can’t directly perceive anything in this world without light coming into them
and providing a picture on the retina
for our brain to interpret.

Thus, while you have the light,
believe what you have seen.

There will come a day
when that light will no longer be available,
and you will be lost in darkness
if you didn’t walk the narrow road
when you had the chance.

This is one of many examples throughout the Bible where we are taught not merely to take hold
of one aspect of God’s nature at a time,
but to hold together in our understanding
all aspects of his nature at all times.

Theology must not be haphazardly crafted
from fragmented doctrines,
but drawn out of Scripture
with open-minded submission
to the Holy Spirit’s inspired
complementary explanations
found within Scripture itself.

God’s grace does not exist in isolation.
It exists in the light of his justice, mercy, and love.

Next,
let’s re-examine John c. 6 v. 47.

Only a few sentences after Jesus’s comment
regarding the Father drawing people to Jesus,
He then says the following in v. 47
pertaining to belief in Himself:
“whoever believes has eternal life”,
implying that whoever does not believe
will not have eternal life.

This moves the **emphasis** from the giver
to the receiver —
from the **gifted** **nature** of Faith
to the **responsibility** of **choosing** to receive it.

Nevertheless,
that shift of emphasis is not due to contradiction,
but seemingly in order to communicate
an urgent exhortation for proper response.

If we have a responsibility to respond with belief,
then it seems safe to say that it’s more important
for us to understand and accept that fact
than it is for us to understand
how Faith mechanistically functions as a gift.

While both concepts are necessary for a **completed** and **perfect** theology of Faith,
it is not actually our first priority
to achieve the full wisdom of God
to understand all things perfectly.

His thoughts are higher than our thoughts,
his mind as unknowable as the vastness of creation!

Our need is not perfected understanding of all things,
as only God has,
but sufficient understanding to repent and believe.

If all one hears is that Faith is a gift
and they are not told
that they need to respond and believe in the Gospel, then they might just respond with something like,
“oh, that’s neat”,
and then shelve it in the back of their mind
where they will do nothing with it,
at least not anytime soon.

Our responsibility for response
is the reason for the urgency
in the frequent command to believe.

When Jesus says,
“whoever believes has eternal life”,
He is not describing bare mental assent.

The context makes that clear.

This belief is connected with coming to Christ, receiving life from Christ,
feeding upon Christ as the bread of life,
and being raised by Christ on the last day.

So,
the belief Jesus calls for
is not mere acknowledgment of a fact,
but trusting reception of Him.

The distinction between faithful belief
and mere mental assent
is critically important because,
as it says in James 2:19,
“even the demons believe — and shudder!”

Obviously,
not all categories of belief lead to saving Faith.

Otherwise, the demons who shudder in fear of God would be saved as well,
and we know that this is not the case
because their destiny is the lake of fire.

The difference between the one described in John 6:47 and the demons described in James 2:19 is this:

Belief that merely causes you to shudder in fear
merely acknowledges the reality
of a coming punishment.

Belief that results
in one entrusting their spirit to Christ
leads to saving Faith.

This interpretation of “belief”
is consistent with its use in other Scriptures as well.

One particularly interesting reference for this topic,
I think, is Mark c. 9 v. 23
where Jesus said to the father of a boy
possessed by an evil spirit
that “all things are possible for one who believes”.

The Greek text in that passage
that the ESV translates to “for one who believes” contains the same Greek verb
and very similar grammatical construction
that is present in John 6:47.

Yet,
what else do we read in v. 24
about the belief of the boy’s father
just before Jesus cleanses the boy:
“I believe; help my unbelief!”

This should be a great comfort to everyone
who ever struggles with doubt after coming to Jesus, which seems to be quite a common experience.

While the boy’s father still had doubt,
he committed to trust in the Lord,
and Jesus’s response shows us that this kind of belief is enough for Him to act upon,
even if it doesn’t demonstrate the full confidence
that God deserves.

While this text isn’t explicitly about spiritual salvation in the same sense as John 6:47,
it’s still using the same Greek to talk about belief
that leads to miraculous intervention from Jesus
to cleanse an unclean spirit.

With this passage in particular,
it would not make sense to interpret belief as coerced since the father of the boy expressed doubt
even in the act of committing to trust Jesus
to save his son.

He believed because Jesus revealed Himself
as the Lord,
capable and worthy of Faith,
and Jesus vindicated that belief with a miracle,
despite the obvious presence of doubt.

The only way to support an interpretation
of coerced belief from this passage
would be to say that God forced the man to believe
**and** doubt simultaneously.

While we could say
that God is capable of such a thing,
I cannot fathom a justification for thinking
that this is consistent with God’s revealed character
and the greater narrative of Scripture,
or even the plethora of anecdotal evidence
that is available from the personal testimonies
that I’ve witnessed.

Let’s briefly consider a few more examples.

Mark c. 1 v. 14-15 says:

Jesus came into Galilee,
proclaiming the Gospel of God,
and saying “the time is fulfilled,
and the kingdom of God is at hand;
repent and believe in the Gospel.”

End quote.

This is Christ’s simple repeated command:
repent and believe in the Gospel —
meaning turn away from your sins
and have faith in God for salvation
through the death and resurrection of the Son of Man: Jesus Christ!

Acts c. 17 v. 30-31 says:

The times of ignorance God overlooked,
but now He commands all people everywhere
to repent,
31 because He has fixed a day

End quote.

God has proven the identity of Christ
through the resurrection
as the one who will judge the world,
and calls us to repent
before the coming Day of the Lord.

Once again,
we wouldn’t have the Gospel
without grace paving the way for Faith,
but we can’t avoid the responsibility
implicit in the command to repent and believe!

In Acts c. 2 v. 38 during Peter’s sermon at Pentecost, he said to the crowd:

“Repent and be baptized every one of you
in the name of Jesus Christ
for the forgiveness of your sins,
and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.”

End quote.

Again,
we see that when people learn who Jesus is
and what He has done for them,
and they ask what they should do,
the answer is to take action —
to respond appropriately,
which is to repent, believe,
and receive the Holy Spirit of God —
to proclaim to the world through baptism
in the name of Jesus Christ
that you will be found in Him
on the day of resurrection
because his life is now your life!

Notice that in the next verse
Peter said that the promise of God for salvation
is for “everyone whom the Lord our God calls
to Himself”,
and earlier in v. 21 he quoted Joel 2:32 from the OT,
which says,

“it shall come to pass
that everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.”

That dynamic describes a call and response
that doesn’t fit well with the idea of an irresistible draw that some are granted
while others are inexplicably left to perish for lack of.

That assessment is further strengthened
by Joshua c. 24 v. 14-15, which says:

“Now therefore fear the Lord
and serve Him in sincerity and in faithfulness.

Put away the gods that your fathers served
beyond the River and in Egypt,

**15** If it is evil in your eyes to serve the Lord,

End quote.

How could we choose what master we will serve
if God’s call is selective and irresistible?

How could we serve God with sincerity
if it is effectively forced?

This verse affirms the free choice of will
to choose what master we will serve,
and implicitly refers to the type of relationship
that God wants with his people:
one of sincere love
rather than puppeteer reaction or robotic servitude.

That doesn’t negate our reliance on God
for providing the call.

It acknowledges the reality
that He illuminates the narrow road
and awaits our choice to walk it with Him,
though He already knows what choice
each of us will make.

I’d like to acknowledge that I can’t consider this

I could walk through that passage phrase-by-phrase
to explain it,
but that’s not particularly necessary in this instance because v. 17 interprets what is needed for us,
and as we should acknowledge,
the ultimate interpretation of Scripture

since all Scripture is the inspired Word of God.

V. 17 says that Faith comes from hearing,
and hearing through the word of Christ.

This is why the Gospel must be preached,
because the reality is that if no one preaches it
then no one hears it;
and if no one hears it,
then no one believes it.

You can dismiss that if you want by saying
that God can preach just fine on his own.

He can call directly and effectively
anyone that He chooses,
to which I’ll concur.

Yes, indeed.
He **can** call out directly to anyone whom He chooses,
effectively replacing the need for human preaching.

He can speak with a voice like thunder
or call to us in our dreams
and all the world could hear the Gospel of Christ without a preacher ever taking the stage.

The problem is,
that’s not what God tells us to presume He **will** do,
**and** it contradicts what He has **commanded** **us** to do.

People need to hear the Gospel,
and God wants us to do his will
by witnessing to them on his behalf.

You don’t have to understand why this is true
in order to act on it.

You just have to believe his Word and obey.

In some instances, He **may** directly intervene
and reveal Himself miraculously, even by name
to those who have not heard the Gospel
from human lips,
similarly to his known prior miraculous revelations
throughout human history,
but we can’t use that as an excuse
to neglect the command to be his hands and feet —
to neglect the desperate need of those
who are not ready for the second coming
because they haven’t heard the good news yet.

V. 8-17 are supported shortly thereafter by v. 21,

“All day long I have held out my hands
to a disobedient and contrary people.”

I appreciate this verse because it follows
right after emphasizing our responsibility
to preach the Gospel
so that people can receive the knowledge of truth
to remind us that God’s hand
is never removed from his people.

While He commands us to preach,
He Himself continues to reach out his hands
towards a people that are disobedient
and contrary to Him.

The second verse that I want to examine
prior to Paul’s message in 2 Timothy
is from Paul’s message in 1 Timothy c. 2 v. 3-4,
which says,
“God our Savior… desires all people to be saved

Again,
the message is plain and simple,
so that none of us should miss it.

God desires that everyone learns the truth
that leads to salvation,
and as we just read from Paul’s earlier letter to Rome, that requires the preaching of the Gospel
according to the will of God.

Now, finally,
let’s consider the later passage in 2 Timothy c. 2 v. 25.

First, I’ll read just the particular phrase within v. 25
that Reformed theologians are likely to lean on
so that it’s clear how easily one can arrive
at their typical conclusion.

Following that, I want to explain the potential issues with allowing that phrase in isolation
to settle the doctrinal question,
measure our expectations for remaining mystery,
and finally,
consider the greater context of the verse itself.

This will require that we consider not only
the immediate context of the statement in v. 24-26,
but also that we direct our attention
to the apparent purpose of the entire passage
so that we can allow the immediate context
to help clarify the bounds of our interpretive liberty with the isolated phrase in question.

The key phrase is the following excerpt
from v. 25 which says,
“God may perhaps grant them repentance
leading to a knowledge of the truth.”

It’s easy to see how this verse
could lead one to the conclusion
that God effectively grants repentance
directly to people
rather than allowing it to happen
functionally of their own free will.

The primary issue that I and others
hold against this interpretation
is that it is **functionally** **equivalent**
to God forcibly overriding human will
in a matter that He also seems to clearly affirm
the responsibility of our own will to make a decision.

A Reformed theologian may say
that even if God does grant repentance directly through effectually willing it,
that wouldn’t contradict free will **because**
they find both the responsibility of creaturely will
and the effectual will of God in Scripture.

Essentially,
because they see both concepts decreed by God,
they must both be true.

The risk with accepting this reasoning **too quickly**
is that by that method of arguing
one could hypothetically try to justify
practically any manner of false doctrine
while **evading** critique
by simply claiming that it is derived from Scripture,
so even if it seems to contradict something else
that one also claims to derive from Scripture,
they’re both true anyway.

I have great respect
for the apparent humility of a person to say
that even if it makes no sense to them,
they will believe what God decrees.

That said,
prior to that humble acceptance,
I think the reasoning of the conclusion in question
is insufficient,
so any humble bowing out of further consideration
seems an acceptance of more troubling mystery
than Scripture genuinely enforces on us in this case.

God doesn’t owe us the answer to every question,
but He has provided his Word to read,
his Spirit to guide,
and our minds to consider
as we reason through the evidence.

Furthermore,
as I said near the beginning of this episode,
Mark 12:30 tells us to love God
not only with our strength, soul, and heart,
but also with our God-given mind.

Therefore, I propose
that to love God with all of our mind indicates
that we should **strive** to ensure that we have achieved
our best reasonable understanding
of what He has provided prior to holding
seemingly contradictory statements together
through premature acceptance of mystery
when a shift in perspective may reveal
the greater fullness of truth that was missing.

In my view,
Reformed thought, among others,
regarding Faith and grace
stops **just** short of that.

That’s not to say that Reformed theologians
lack intelligence or sincerity,
just that even careful human argumentation
can be influenced by the fallibility of humanity.

I’ll discuss later in this episode
the degree of mystery that seems truly unavoidable, and to that I will **absolutely** commend humility
as our response to God’s **unfathomable** wisdom —

but first, let’s consider whether we can derive
any further clarity in Scripture.

I will admit
that the danger of human pride in such circumstances
is that I might wrongly think myself
to have the superior reasoning,
and so wrongly believe that my argument
**must** be the correct one.

Thus, I might discard whatever doesn’t seem
to be explained by my reasoning,
or just consider it part of the remaining mystery
that **can’t** be explained within the bounds
of human limitations
of knowledge and comprehension.

That is why I did my best in preparing for this episode, to ensure that my presuppositions
did not pre-determine my conclusions
rather than allowing the reasoning of Scripture itself
to guide my understanding —

and in consideration of that,
I am all the more grateful to God
that my salvation has never hinged
on my perfect understanding of all things,
but only on Faith in Christ
through which I repent and believe in the Gospel.

All that said,
my issue with conclusions that inform a soteriology,
or theology of salvation,
that require an effectual will
which results in irresistible grace **selectively** offered
is that I think it causes
more apparent mystery and confusion
in regards to the interpretation of Scripture
and the character of God
than the alternative that I’m presenting
in this exploration of the theology of Faith.

That is because the Reformed doctrine
seems to require contradictions
in the inerrant Word of God,
whereas my current understanding
of the apparently more biblically sound alternative
seems to not require any such contradictions.

I don’t want to accidentally affirm false doctrines through appeals to mystery.

I would rather say confidently
that I can reconcile the evidence
without apparently necessary remaining contradiction,
or else simply say that I don’t understand it yet,
if I ever will.

To those who have witnessed it,
I’ve proven in the past
that I’m willing to change my mind
with sufficient evidence,
and I’m also willing to accept
whatever degree of mystery that God requires of me.

In fact, I’ve even refined my position
regarding this very doctrine in the not so recent past
in such a way that I would not now describe Faith
the way that I might have in the past.

I recall a memory that I believe
to be from my time in graduate school
in which I described faith to a friend
summarily as our contribution
to the equation for salvation.

While I’m confident that I can qualify that in a way that makes it true in a sense,
I think that the conclusion that it seemed to affirm
for my friend at that time was human sufficiency
in contradiction to the gifted nature of biblical Faith.

With that methodological caution in mind,
let’s return to Paul’s 2nd letter to Timothy, c. 2
and consider v. 24-26 now.

The Scripture says:

… the Lord’s servant must not be quarrelsome
but kind to everyone, able to teach,
patiently enduring evil,
25 correcting his opponents with gentleness.

God may perhaps grant them repentance
leading to a knowledge of the truth,
26 and they may come to their senses
and escape from the snare of the devil,
after being captured by him to do his will.

End quote.

What is the purpose of this passage?

Paul is explaining from the beginning of v. 24
through beginning of v. 25
how a servant of God must fulfill his role.

Consider the following **paraphrase** of those verses
in my own words
which I think conveys the point clearly.

The Lord’s servant must be a good teacher
who corrects people gently.

God may blaze a trail to repentance
by which they may escape the snare of the Devil
and receive the knowledge of truth.

This entire chapter of 2 Timothy
is focused on how to **identify**
or **be** a leader in the Church,
so why would Paul say in v. 25
right after explaining that the Lord’s servants
should be good teachers
that God may grant people repentance
leading to knowledge of truth?

This is why I wanted to consider the earlier passages in Romans 10 and 1 Timothy prior to 2 Timothy.

1 Timothy 2:4 reveals
that God desires for all people to be saved
and come to knowledge of the truth.

2 Timothy 2:25 comments
that God may grant repentance
leading to the knowledge of the truth.

Therefore, God’s granting of repentance
must be considered and understood
in harmonious conjunction
with His revealed desire that all be saved
so that **our** interpretation
doesn’t contradict **his** message,
and Romans c. 10 shows us
how this works out practically:

God provides grace through Christ.
Christ lives out the Gospel.
The Gospel is preached to sinners.
Sinners hear the truth,
and some repent and believe,
leading to salvation.

By this way,
Faith comes by hearing,
and God grants repentance.

For the sake of those who might find themselves stuck on what I would consider
an unnecessarily narrow interpretation
of God’s granting,
allow me to paint a clear metaphorical picture
to explain my understanding of granting **in general**.

I could fairly say
that a parent **grants** their child ice-cream,
even if the child retrieves the ice-cream themselves from the freezer,
as long as the parent
had to allow the child the opportunity
such as by purchasing it from the store,
producing it from scratch,
or even just **telling** the child where to find it.

My confidence in the interpretation
of God’s granting of Faith that I’ve presented
comes not just from how well it explains
each proof-text individually,
but more importantly
how it allows them to speak not just as individuals,
but as a choir proclaiming a unified Gospel of truth.

That unity of purpose and message
is precisely what I would expect
from an omniscient, sovereign God,
and that’s what I find in Scripture.

Furthermore,
I propose that this understanding explains
why God emphasizes throughout the Scriptures
that we receive the gift of Faith
rather than accept that we may or may not
be effectually willed to receive it.

God **grants** what He commands
by means of his gracious initiative
to provide a path to salvation and light the way for us.

Since I’ve been addressing ideas and theological camps
without naming any specific instances
of an individual’s actual defense of them,
I’m now going to pivot for a few minutes
to address more directly certain names and arguments pertaining to the debate of prevenient grace
(grace that precedes and enables a genuine response) versus irresistible grace
(grace that effectually secures the desired response).

The reason that I ultimately decided
this is a worthwhile addition to this episode
is that it seemed to me
that any thorough theology of Faith
necessarily becomes also a theology of God’s will:
what He commands, what He desires,
what He enables,
and what He effectually causes in a direct manner.

Essentially, my methodology
for certain apparent issues of clarity in Scripture
is, in a sense,
to treat it similarly to an algebra problem.

I almost can’t believe
that I’m actually using algebra right now,
let alone in the context of theological methodology.

Nevertheless, consider this approach:

If the doctrine of Faith is one variable,
the doctrine of God’s will is another variable,
and the final theological conclusion is the result
that Scripture requires us to preserve
when combining those variables,
then we should use the clearest biblical data pertaining to those variables
to help us understand what is less immediately clear.

In this instance,
I would propose that the doctrine of Faith
and the revealed contours of God’s nature,
purposes, and character
are more plainly clarified in Scripture
than the hidden mechanics of God’s will.

Therefore, 
we should allow the clarity
pertaining to Faith and God’s other aspects
to enlighten us regarding his will.

Essentially,
if Scripture speaks clearly
about Faith being commanded and grace-enabled,
and also speaks clearly about God’s justice,
mercy, love, and sincerity,
then those truths must constrain
how we understand the more mysterious workings
of God’s will.

To simplify this methodology,
if a+b=c,
and I have a greater degree of certainty
regarding ‘a’ and ‘c’,
then I can use that information
to inform my evaluation of ‘b’.

Methodology aside,
I will say that I have greatly appreciated most
of what I’ve heard and read from Dr. John Piper,
but on this particular topic,
my conclusions will align more closely
to the Arminian soteriology
that he was arguing against
in the book chapter that I am going to reference
for this discussion.

That chapter is available
in the form of an article on desiringgod.org titled
“Are There Two Wills in God?” from 1995.

By the way,
I always include lists
of primary and secondary references when appropriate in my YT video descriptions,
so if you would like to examine those
more conveniently for yourself,
you can refer to the corresponding
YT video’s description for now.

At a yet undetermined time in the future,
I will have a website

For now,
let’s return to the topic at hand,
the aforementioned article pertaining to God’s will.

In it, Dr. Piper said the following:

We know it was *not* the "will of God"
that Judas and Pilate and Herod
and the Gentile soldiers and the Jewish crowds disobey the moral law of God by sinning
in delivering Jesus up to be crucified.

But we also know that it *is* the will of God
that this came to pass.

Therefore, we know that God
in some sense
wills what he does not will in another sense.

End quote.

I agree with the observation that God can,
in some sense, will that certain things occur
while not willing them in another sense.

What I disagree with is the conclusion
that this logically requires multiple wills in God.

I propose instead that God has a single unified will with ordered priorities
by which He may account for and permit
what He does not morally delight in
because it serves the greater purpose
that He truly wills.

To be fair to Dr. Piper,
I don’t think that his conclusions
at the time that he wrote that article
were completely misguided.

In fact, I think that he did an excellent job
of explaining his case,
and I can clearly see how he drew his conclusions.

I also can’t say for certain
that Dr. Piper hasn’t changed his position
by the time that I’m evaluating this writing
from over 30 years ago,
but I suspect it wouldn’t still be
on his ministry’s website if he had.

Even if he hasn’t, I believe
that were we to converse together for long enough
then we would find that we agreed
on far more than we disagreed.

Nevertheless, regarding this matter,
it is my opinion
that the conclusions presented
in the aforementioned article
are what I refer to as “almost, but not quite”.

I’ll explain why shortly,
and it’s not because I think I’m smarter than Dr. Piper.

I don’t think that I’m presenting any new
or novel theology or doctrine in this episode.

The only novelty that I’m trying to bring
to the discussion is my own voice, perspective,
and way of explaining the eternal truths of God
that have always been and always will be.

Times and cultures may change,
but God remains the same.

That’s not because He’s stubborn or close-minded,
but because He has always been all-knowing
and perfectly holy,
so there is no reason for Him to change —
no better version for Him to become.
He already is perfect and sufficient in all regards.

That said,
Consider now my reasoning
for why Dr. Piper’s ultimate conclusion
of the two-will nature of God is
“almost, but not quite”.

First, I want to draw attention to the reality
that Scripture allows us to distinguish
between what God commands,
what God permits,
and what God brings to pass.

However, those distinctions must never be defined
in a way that makes God contradict Himself,
because as it says in Ephesians c. 1 v. 11,
God “works all things
according to the counsel of his will”,
and as it says in 2 Timothy c. 2 v. 13,
“He cannot deny Himself”.

In the aforementioned article,
Dr. Piper says the following:

“Affirming the will of God to save *all*, 
while also affirming the unconditional election of *some*, implies that there are at least "two wills" in God,
or two ways of willing.”

End quote.

To take this statement as it is **by itself**
would be to start from the presupposition
of unconditional election that effects faith
through irresistible grace —
the theology that he is defending in this excerpt —
and use it to determine an aspect of God’s nature.

That’s fine
**if** you’ve proven the doctrine of unconditional election from Scripture,
but if there’s a more reasonable interpretation
of the Scriptures used to support that doctrine,
then any inference from that doctrine
about the nature of God is suspect.

However, instead of merely deconstructing
Dr. Piper’s explanation outright,
what we first have to do
is affirm what is known about God
from the plain reading of Scripture,
what is reasonable according to his revelation
as we possess it,
and then either use that
to inform our developing theology,
or compare it to our established theology
with the intention to throw out
any doctrine that doesn’t fit.

I’m not saying that Dr. Piper reversed this process,
as I have suspected some other people of doing,
but I do think that there is another perspective informed by responsible exegesis
that looks beyond the isolated proof texts
to the metanarrative of Scripture,
as well as reasonable philosophical observations
of God’s creation.

As a disclaimer,
I would like to say that if terms
obviously related to “philosophy”
are a concerning sign to you,
please understand
that what I consider good philosophy
is the study of the nature of knowledge, reality,
and existence
informed by the revelation of God
through creation, his Word,
and miraculous personal experiences **from God**,
which of course cannot truly contradict
his other forms of revelation,
due to his unchanging consistency.

I’m not talking about anti-religious
or pseudo-intellectual so-called philosophy
that you may hear from people who try
and inevitably fail to wield academia
as an eraser against spiritual reality.

Digression aside,
the theologians that I’ve read and listened to
discuss this topic often use terminology
such as revealed versus secret
or preceptive versus decretive
that seems to suggest that Scripture forces us
to one of two conclusions.

One of those being
that He can **desire** things that He does not **will**,
which I think is not a careful use of words
with consideration for their definitions.

The other is that God has a will for what He desires
that is functionally **distinct** or effectively **separate**
from what He actually wills **effectually**,
which seems like competing wills
in every way that I’ve seen or considered it explained,
although I am well aware
that those holding to traditional Reformed theology
will disagree on that characterization.

I just don’t think that their explanations
can avoid adopting that characterization
by virtue of their necessary implications.

Nevertheless,
this seems to be the two-will theory
that Dr. Piper supported in the aforementioned article.

Since I’m referring to specific use
of certain terms here,
I would like to take some time
to discuss the use of a certain term within a quotation that I found elsewhere in the article
where Dr. Piper referred to a statement
by Dr. Ian Howard Marshall 
from Pinnock’s “Case for Arminianism”.

To be clear,
I haven’t read the full original context
of the statement in its source material,
only its use in the aforementioned article by Dr. Piper.

As such,
I don’t intend to comment
on Dr. Marshall’s intended meaning or argument.

Instead,
I’m going to intentionally focus
on the wording of the isolated statement as quoted
to demonstrate how even **fair** **use** of a quotation
can provide an opportunity
for **unintended** interpretations
that may be plausible due to either
suboptimal wording
or simply lacking the necessary context
of the source material to clarify the **intended** meaning.

That said,
the primary conclusion that I intend to support
for the purpose of this episode
with the results of my analysis
is that we can’t use the excuse
that it’s **merely** a matter of semantics
to avoid civil discourse regarding this doctrine.

That only works
when the difference in semantics doesn’t change
the plausible meaningful outcomes
or theological implications
of the doctrine.

In this case,
I think that the quotation from Dr. Marshall,
in isolation as presented by Dr. Piper,
can be an example of how the semantics of a phrase can accidentally contribute to confusion in discussion, especially when it pertains to the systematic workings of a theological concept
where God hasn’t necessarily given us
the faculties and/or data
to comprehensively comprehend
the inner mechanisms of those theological systems —
and to be clear,
I think that Faith qualifies
to be considered within that category.

I would also like to note
that despite calling this a critique of the quote
**as presented** in Dr. Piper’s work,
I’m not claiming that he **unfairly** used the quote.

I merely think that separated from its context,
as quotes often are,
the range of plausible interpretation
from what little text is given
provides an opportunity to make a relevant point.

If this begins to seem an overlong excursus
from the main topic of discussion,
bear with me until I have the chance to demonstrate how this line of reasoning has informed my approach to developing and presenting this theology of Faith.

I’m aware that the following critique
might at first seem like nitpicking,
but try to consider with me the potential implications of what I’m going to draw attention to
under the **hypothetical** circumstance
that the **original context**
did **not** clarify the quoted statement effectively.

To understand the critique,
what I want to draw attention to
is Dr. Marshall’s particular way of using the term “will” in the following quotation.

It says:

“We must certainly distinguish
between what God **would like to see** happen
and what He **actually does will** to happen.”

End quote.

I think that the presented use of the term “will”
in isolation could be considered not careful enough
to prevent a certain plausible misunderstanding.

My reasoning is
that the way this sentence reads out of context
easily fits a careless philosophy of will
which might claim that desire with infinite power
requires perfected short-term outcomes
according to each individual desire.

Not only would that be a logical problem,
since desiring two opposing outcomes from the same situation would result in a genuine paradox,
it would also be a theological problem,
because it would seem to require
that God does
what He Himself says that He cannot do,
which is to contradict Himself.

That conclusion would be genuinely required according to the careless philosophy of will
that I’m describing, because,
as Dr. Piper pointed out in the aforementioned article,
we have clear indication in Scripture
that God can in some sense will something
that He also does not will in another sense —
a prime example being
the murder of Jesus Christ on the cross.

God does not delight in evil,
yet in some sense it was his will,
as revealed in Scripture,
that Christ die on the cross for the extraordinary redemption arc of humanity.

What He had delight in
was the good outcome
that He had planned from the beginning
which accounted for the evil that would occur,
which is morally distinct from delighting in evil itself.

In concluding my analysis

What **I** am referring to as “logic” in this context

By that definition,

The very idea cannot even be described sufficiently

We may be able

those to whom the blood of the Lamb is applied.

Though I am ethnically a Gentile,
I have been spiritually adopted into the people of Israel with all other true believers
because there is no Jew or Gentile in Christ!

And to that I say, hallelujah and amen!

Finally,
I have but one more
supposed kryptonite of prevenient grace to identify
so that I can explain why
it actually complements my theology
better than it may at first seem
depending on how you may have been conditioned
to understand it.

In Ephesians c. 1 v. 3-14
we have one of the grandest depictions
of our grafting into the body of Christ,
which is admittedly considered by some
to be strong evidence against the model of Faith
that I’m ultimately supporting in this episode.

After I read it,
I’ll explain why I think it’s not
the problem for prevenient grace
that some people have made it out to be,
and that understanding comes largely
due to the recurring themes that I discussed
regarding the last several biblical references.

Consider this especially beautiful
passage of Scripture with me:

Blessed be the God and Father
of our Lord Jesus Christ,
who has blessed us in Christ
with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, 4 even as He chose us in Him
before the foundation of the world,
that we should be holy and blameless before Him.

In love 5 He predestined us for adoption to Himself
as sons through Jesus Christ,
according to the purpose of his will,
6 to the praise of his glorious grace,
with which He has blessed us in the Beloved.

7In Him we have redemption through his blood,
the forgiveness of our trespasses,
according to the riches of his grace,
8 which He lavished upon us, in all wisdom and insight 9 making known to us the mystery of his will, according to his purpose, which He set forth in Christ 10 as a plan for the fullness of time,
to unite all things in Christ,
things in heaven and things on earth in Him.

11 In Him we have obtained an inheritance,
having been predestined
according to the purpose of Him
who works all things according to the counsel
of his will,
12 so that we who were the first to hope in Christ
might be to the praise of his glory.

13 In Him you also, when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed in Him,
were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit,
14 who is the guarantee of our inheritance
until we acquire possession of it,
to the praise of his glory.

End quote.

Notice again
that Scripture explains exactly how it is
that we are chosen
by revealing that believers are chosen in Christ.

I believe that I’ve already established
through the previous examples
how that fact reveals the means
by which election language is ultimately
and finally applied in the fullest sense
as God intended.

All people are blessed through Abram, Jacob,
and Israel because of God the Son
who was incarnated as the God-Man
into the bloodline of Abram
to offer adoption into the covenant people of God
to **all** people.

Now,
I should discuss the obvious word in this translation that I’m sure every proponent of irresistible grace would latch onto,
that being the word, “predestined”.

What does it mean that God predestined
those found in Christ for adoption?

However we interpret that idea,
if we choose to do the work of interpretation at all
as opposed to simply following God’s clear commands
and considering the rest a mystery,
we must be consistent with everything else
that is revealed in Scripture,
because Scripture is the Word of God,
and therefore cannot be in contradiction with itself.

It would be easy to say
that the presence of the word “predestined”
must imply effectual will and irresistible grace,
but I think yet again there is a simple explanation
that fits more reasonably with God’s character
and nature as revealed in Scripture.

First, note that in Ephesians,
Paul did not speak of predestination
as an abstract mechanism detached from Christ.

Rather,
Paul says that we are chosen in Him, Jesus Christ,
and predestined for adoption **through** **Christ**
so that those who **believe** in Him
would be sealed with the Holy Spirit,
as it says in v. 13.

Within this divinely inspired text,
Paul guarded against a misinterpretation
of predestination by providing the condition of belief within the immediate context.

Thus,
it seems more probable than plausible
that we should understand God’s predestination
of all believers
as God’s eternal purpose to save those who would,
as it says in the very same passage,
believe in Christ from hearing the Gospel,
and be adopted into the chosen people —
the New Jerusalem —
the body of Christ.

The fact that God describes his choosing
of those found **in** Christ
harmonizes beautifully with his description
of the Church as the body of Christ—
those who “put on Christ”
as we just read in Galatians 3:27.

It can be tempting to treat a word as the key
that unlocks a verse,
or a verse as the key that unlocks a passage,
but if that’s the only approach that we use
for interpretation
then we are blinding ourselves to the clarity
that context provides for those key verses and words.

Stated simply,
we can’t specify the scope
of a word’s intended meaning
without considering the implicit defining limitations
of its related context.

This entire theological model of will, grace, and Faith also aligns harmoniously with God’s goodness
and purpose for genuine loving relationship,
which is a critical belief for the Christian Faith,
because as God Himself says,
He truly is Love, and He truly does love us
even while we were yet still unclean sinners.

Obviously,
God’s omniscience requires that He foreknows
who will respond obediently with Faith.

Yet,
the emphasis of even this passage
which some might consider a pillar
of Reformed soteriology
is not on some mysterious hidden mechanism
that irresistibly causes faith.

Rather,
the emphasis is on the eternal plan of God
fulfilled in Christ
and applied to those who hear the Gospel,
believe the truth,
and respond faithfully
to receive the seal of the Holy Spirit.

The emphasis is on our reception of the gift of Faith more so than the gifted nature of Faith.

While I think that all I’ve discussed thus far
is sufficient to make the necessary points
for this section
pertaining to the biblical emphasis on reception,
I’d like to make a final, perhaps relatively minor,
point of philosophical conjecture.

This theory is less concrete by far
than most of my other lines of reasoning,
but I haven’t been able to disprove it to myself
thus far,
so it seems to me to remain an interesting possibly
at the very least.

I’m also not certain whether I’m the first person
to try to explain it exactly this way,
so if this idea is too novel for comfort,
please, take it with a grain of salt.

That said, here’s my theory.

Because God is eternal,
we know that He is not bound by the limits of time, which is how He can be truly omniscient.

Since we know that He is eternal and omniscient,
we can also know that He has always known
who would respond to his grace faithfully
and take part in the salvation offered through Christ.

Therefore,
I believe it fair to propose that in some sense
He could say that He predestines those
who will respond with Faith
by the very act of choosing to create them.

In essence,
knowing what will happen
as a consequence of an action
followed by choosing to perform that action
is in some sense predetermining the outcome.

Of course, this logic doesn’t work
if you assert a very limited definition of predestination which requires **directly** manipulating the variables
at all times to enforce the outcome,
rather than including the possibility
of making strategically planned choices
that are known to lead to the desired outcome.

However,
I believe that I can fairly use the term this way because I think that to define predestination in a way that necessarily disproves this hypothesis
by definition alone would require affirming conclusions that I have already through this episode labored
to sufficiently counter with better explanations.

To further explain my meaning
with a non-supernatural example
that mirrors imagery used by Jesus Himself
as recorded in the Gospel pertaining to salvation,
I could say the following:

If you are certain
that a person will freely accept an invitation
to your wedding if it is genuinely offered,
and you then choose to send that invitation,
you have in some sense predetermined, or predestined, that the person will be present at the wedding
without having to coerce
or force them through the door.

Knowing what choice another will make
in response to your offer does not mean
that your predestination of the outcome
is forcibly deterministic
like the theoretical mechanism of irresistible grace is,
as long as the person still has the genuine ability
to reject the offer.

You are simply effecting your will
through taking the steps that you know
will lead to the desired outcome.

Likewise,
God has effected his will to save the body of Christ
by taking the steps that He knew would result
in his chosen people being found in Christ
in order to predestine them for salvation.

If all of that seems like nonsense to you,
that’s alright.

We don’t have to agree on the mechanistic explanation of this particular matter
to follow the explicit direct commands of God
and be confident that we will have
all of our theology worked out together
after we are resurrected
into the coming perfect eternity in the presence of God.

In fact, as I implied earlier,
I believe that if we simply focus
on the clear commands of God in Scripture
and committed all else that remains
to be considered unattainable mystery in this life,
then we would still have everything that we **need**
for practical daily living and salvation
according to God’s Gospel of Faith in Christ.

That’s not going to stop me, personally,
from attempting to seek the more elusive wisdoms
and mysteries of Scripture,
but I won’t pretend that everyone else in the world
is required to dive this deep into the weeds
or agree with all of my particular conclusions.

If at any point after this you decide
that you will hold to the doctrine of irresistible grace
in spite of the case that I or anyone else may present for a different theological model,
then I would finally at least beg you to remember
to be encouraged by the assurance
that this is not an issue
which I would consider breaking fellowship over.

Furthermore,
as Dr. John Piper has suggested
in some of the material that I studied for this project
and in accordance with James 4:12,
it is not our place to know who is elected.

Even if you believe in an effectual calling
that is applied selectively by irresistible grace
without the functionally present ability to resist through independent will,
you should not try to **determine for yourself**
who may or may not be called.

You should not pick and choose
to whom the Gospel should be shared —
to whom should be evangelized or discipled —
based on your own judgement
of who might be one of the elect or chosen.

It’s not our job to judge
whether a person is one of the elect.

That’s for God and God alone to judge.

There is but one Lawgiver and Judge,
and you are not Him.

To conclude this section
in which I’ve attempted to explain
the emphasis on reception of the gift of Faith
found throughout Scripture,
I will note that while I think the effectual will
and irresistible grace doctrines
are inconsistent with God’s expressed desire
that all know the truth,
his universal call to Faith,
the Great Commission,
and the metanarrative of Scripture in general,
I know that my Reformed
brothers and sisters in Christ
generally still affirm the unambiguous command
to preach the Gospel to all people,
to disciple those who choose to believe,
and to leave the rest to God.

That’s enough for me to cross the aisle on this matter and cooperate with our shared purpose
to go forth and do the will of God,
however it is that we think his will is effectively applied within our experience of time.

## ***PROXIMITY DOES NOT IMPART FAITH***

Before I address works and boasting,
I want to first pivot to a brief discussion of proximity
or association.

Most people
who have attended a local church body very often probably recognize that there is a seemingly
ever-present category of people
that some have called pew-sitters,
or spiritually lukewarm people
who seem to be Christian in name only.

These people tend to attend church services
at least somewhat regularly,
but they also tend to not really immerse themselves
in the life and work of the Church.

However,
what I think is even more concerning than those
whom we may feel relatively confident identifying
are those who put on a show that will fool all
but the very most discerning,
even if they can never fool God
who judges the heart with perfect clarity.

Some, such as Judas Iscariot,
may seem closer to Jesus,
closer to holiness and righteousness
than nearly anyone else you’ve ever witnessed —
until the mask slips
and they are revealed to be a deceiving charlatan —
a betrayer —
an apostate.

I think that Judas is the clearest example 
of someone being called by Jesus to Faith
in every way that we can observe
and yet ultimately choosing to reject the Gospel.

Not only did he receive his call to discipleship
face-to-face with his incarnated God and Messiah,
he walked with Jesus, heard his teachings,
witnessed his miracles firsthand,
shared in the ministry of the original Twelve apostles, and was warned by Jesus Himself
of the impending betrayal, death, and resurrection.

Yet still,
Judas ultimately betrayed Jesus to the cross, committing one of the greatest atrocities
in all of human history
by effectively sending the Messiah of all mankind
to the most humiliating form of tortuous execution
in that historical era.

If Judas the betrayer was not justified
by his position among the disciples of Jesus,
then we must recognize
that church attendance and spiritual titles
cannot impart moral holiness
or Faith unto salvation.

A person can stand near the Light
and still love the darkness —
hear the words of Christ
without trusting the heart of Christ —
participate outwardly with Christian activities
and yet remain inwardly surrendered to their sin.

The example of Judas teaches a sobering reality regarding the God who calls us to Faith.

That is that He does not confuse proximity with union or outward participation with inward surrender.

This lesson emphasizes the need for a genuine transformation of the soul through spiritual rebirth
in response to the call of our Savior.

My primary reason for bringing this to the discussion
is the following cautionary recommendation:

I would urge all to recognize
that any doctrine of Faith
and means of teaching and explaining it
that we subscribe to
must preserve the caution of Judas’s example.

Unfortunately,
I think it’s safe to say
that our churches still regularly shelter people
who are doing little more
than walking their dry bones in and out of the doors without ever drinking from the spring of living water.

You cannot quench your thirst
just by standing near the water,
gazing at your reflection in it,
or even submerging your hand in it.

You have to drink it —
to internalize it.

That also doesn’t make the act of drinking
the saving agent.
That remains with the living water itself.

More on that in the next section.

Before we get to that though,
consider what John c. 1 v. 11-13 says
regarding Jesus Christ our Savior:

He came to his own,
and his own people did not receive Him.

12 But to all who did receive Him,
who believed in his name,
He gave the right to become children of God,
13 who were born, not of blood
nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man,
but of God.

End quote.

It was not those that were considered his people
by proximity or blood
that were given the right to become children of God, but those who **believed** in Him.

Proximity to righteousness does not impart holiness.

Internalizing righteousness through Faith
which leads to the indwelling of the Holy Spirit
is the only means of acquiring righteousness
that justifies one to God
according to his will as revealed in Scripture.

## ***FAITH PRODUCES FRUIT YET EXCLUDES BOASTING***

The final aspect of Faith
that I think needs to be addressed at this point
is the productive nature of Faith
beyond merely the miracle of internal spiritual rebirth leading to ultimate salvation.

That miracle is critically important of course,
but also not the full picture
of Faith’s transformative effects in a believer’s life.

Genuine Faith should also produce external fruit
that can bless those around us
and show them that there is a meaningful difference between a dead life shackled to sin
and the productive freedom that is found in Christ.

That said,
the presence of fruit from good works driven by Faith does not allow us to boast in our works,
which I believe would include the practice
of considering our works in any way contributing
to our justification before God for salvation.

The reality of Faith precluding boasting
testifies of a God
who will not share the glory of salvation
with human merit,
and yet will not leave his people’s lives barren
after spiritual rebirth.

Consider the following a succinct version
of the conclusion that I’m next going
to trace the definition of through Scripture:

If Faith is treated like a trophy
that you earn through your own good works,
it has been misunderstood.

It cannot boast in itself without ceasing to be Faith
as defined by God’s Word.

The fruit of Faith is good works,
beginning with the Holy Spirit’s work in you
which overflows into the rest of your life,
and the fruit that comes from your good works
is reflective of the inherent goodness of God
and his genuine transformation of your life.

Human faith that boasts in itself
has turned its attention from the Giver to the receiver.

Aside from reinforcing the gifted nature of Faith,
this understanding guards
against any doctrine of faith
that regards works as meritorious justification contributing to our salvation.

The identity of Christ applied to us through his blood is the only justification that we need.

It is sufficient to cover all sins of all people,
no matter the timing or severity.

Consider with me
the thesis-containing passage of this topic
found in Ephesians c. 2 v. 8–10.

In this passage, the Word of God says:

… by grace you have been saved through faith.

And this is not your own doing;
it is the gift of God,
9 not a result of works, so that no one may boast.

10 For we are his workmanship,
created in Christ Jesus for good works,
which God prepared beforehand,
that we should walk in them.

End quote.

We are saved by grace through Faith
and yet this is not a result of our work,
but a gift of God
so that no one may boast in themselves,
but only in Christ!

Furthermore,
Scripture does not pit Faith against good works
as though good works are evil.

Truly good works are indeed good by definition,
but not meritorious, or meriting more grace for us because they cannot wash the stain of sin
which requires the blood of Jesus.

What Scripture **does** pit Faith against is boasting, earning, and justification from ourselves.

And as we see in Ephesians 2,
it then immediately tells us
that those saved by grace through Faith
are also created for good works.

Thus,
we must keep the separation clear in our doctrine between what good works do and do not accomplish.

They are the fruit of Faith, not the cause of Faith.

They show people evidence of our salvation,
but they do not earn that salvation.

While I think Ephesians is clear enough
when it says that salvation is not our own doing,
not from our works, and a gift from God,
there’s one slightly more subtle element of proof
hiding in the shadow of those monumental truths.

That is what it **positively** affirms about good works, which is that we are created in Christ for them.

I think it’s clear that “created in Christ”
refers to the spiritual rebirth
which we know is performed by the Holy Spirit
as a consequence of Faith in Christ.

So,
aside from the obvious appeal
to the gifted nature of Faith,
Ephesians also seems to imply
that we are made ready for good works
through being saved —
not partially saved, not saved until we sin again,
but just saved —
by grace through Faith, so that no one may boast.

The force of the position expressed in these Scriptures outweighs any mental gymnastics
that we could perform
in an attempt to understand Faith
as a meritorious work of humanity
in the context of this discussion.

In fact,
if you want to consider Faith as a work,
then according to Scripture
it seems that you must consider it a work of God
freely given to you,
as is well-established by the gifted nature of Faith
that I attempted to explain
in the fourth section of this episode.

I know that some theologians,
especially those attempting to maintain
the Roman Catholic tradition,
have tried to distinguish initial justification
from final justification
in a way that gives human works a role
in our justification unto salvation,
but I just don’t see
how their reasoning can survive the force
of the plain reading of Scriptures
such as Romans 3, Romans 4, and Ephesians 2.

Even when considering the argument
as explained by some Catholic apologists,
the validity of the reasoning seems empowered
not by the unquestionable authority of God’s Word
so much as the human authority of the papacy,
and if you would say
that those are necessarily one and the same,
then I wonder how you can justify the contradiction between the two.

Even Roman Catholic history
as recorded by the Church of Rome itself
contains formally recognized cases
where a pope’s handling of doctrine
was later **condemned** by church authority,
and that condemnation later confirmed
by another pope.

One specific example is Pope Honorius I,
who was anathematized
by the Sixth Ecumenical Council,
which was affirmed by Pope Leo II.

Roman Catholic apologists may point out
that his story does not meet inclusion criteria
for the later definition of an “ex cathedra”
papal statement being recanted,
or more simply an authoritative definition of doctrine meant to bind the entire Church being tossed out,
but that is precisely my point.

Never mind the concern
regarding the claim of divinely appointed authority
for an office
where one can be so wrong in their theology
that they are anathematized
while supposedly remaining infallibly correct
on all specially defined authoritative decrees —

papal explanation and church tradition in any case must not be treated as identical or equal
to the unquestionable authority
of God’s inspired Word.

We must not deviate from the plain truths of Scripture to align with doctrines that seem to require
non-Scriptural sources in order to construct.

Consider with me now Romans c. 3 v. 23-28,
which says:

… all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, 24 and are justified by his grace as a gift,
through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, 25 whom God put forward as a propitiation
by his blood, to be received by faith.

This was to show God’s righteousness,
because in his divine forbearance
He had passed over former sins.

26 It was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that He might be just
and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.

27 Then what becomes of our boasting?
It is excluded.
By what kind of law? By a law of works?
No, but by the law of faith.

28 For we hold that one is justified by faith
apart from works of the law.

End quote.

Consider v. 27 for a moment.

A law of works would allow the possibility
for us to boast in ourselves,
because it would require
that we are saved by our earned righteousness —
our own meritorious works.

The problem with this kind of law
is that it’s the kind of law
which Israel was given in the Old Testament
in order to show us God’s standard and prove for all time that no man will be able to stand
on his own works because,
as it says in the first verse of this reference, v. 23,
“all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God”.

Works only work for whoever has never sinned,
and unless you are Jesus Christ, that is not you.

Therefore,
your only recourse for salvation from sin
is the gift of Faith.

Consider the following comment
from Romans c. 4 v. 1–11:

What then shall we say was gained by Abraham,
our forefather according to the flesh?

2 For if Abraham was justified by works,
he has something to boast about,
but not before God.

3 For what does the Scripture say?
“Abraham believed God,
and it was counted to him as righteousness.”

4 Now to the one who works,
his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due.
5 And to the one who does not work
but believes in Him who justifies the ungodly,
his faith is counted as righteousness,
6 just as David also speaks of the blessing of the one
to whom God counts righteousness apart from works:

7 “Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, 
and whose sins are covered;
8 blessed is the man
against whom the Lord will not count his sin.”

9 Is this blessing then only for the circumcised,
or also for the uncircumcised?

For we say that faith was counted to Abraham
as righteousness.

10 How then was it counted to him?
Was it before or after he had been circumcised?
It was not after, but before he was circumcised.

11 He received the sign of circumcision
as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised.

End quote.

So,
Abraham’s righteousness came from believing God — trusting in God —
having Faith in God.

Faith is not a gift if it is earned,
because what is earned is a wage,
and as it says in v. 5,
the one who does not work but believes in God
is counted as righteous.

There’s no wiggle room in the Bible
for partial justification.
It’s just not in the text.

Abram wasn’t counted partially righteous by belief.
He was counted **righteous**.
Period.

Folks,
this passage precludes any involvement of work
in any kind of salvific justification before God
because it says that the one who does not work
is still considered justified by Faith.

How can one have Faith without performing any work and yet still consider Faith a work?

You cannot,
because it doesn’t logically follow
by any stretch of the imagination
according to the plain biblical definitions.

As was pointed out in v. 11, the sign of Faith,
the work of circumcision in this case,
came **after** Faith was acquired
and considered a seal
of the righteousness imparted by Faith.

A seal is a sign of a pre-existing reality,
not a causal factor of a new reality.

A king does not have authority **because** he has a seal.
His seal has authority because **he** is a **king**.

Consider with me 2 more quick references
to hammer in the final nails in the coffin
of meritorious works for justification
prior to wrapping up with a bow
on the concept of boasting in Christ.

1 Corinthians 1:28–31 says that,

God chose what is low and despised in the world,
even things that are not,
to bring to nothing things that are,
29 so that no human being might boast
in the presence of God.
30 And because of him you are in Christ Jesus,
who became to us wisdom from God,
righteousness and sanctification and redemption,
31 so that, as it is written,
“Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord.”

End quote.

If you think that Christ’s blood is not enough,
and that your good works
are part of your final justification before Christ,
then you are contradicting 1 Corinthians 1:29,
which says that no human will boast
in the presence of God
because if any part of your justification
is by your own merit,
then you would be able to boast on at least that merit.

Furthermore,
this passage also tells us what we **can** boast in
and that is the Lord and Savior —
God the Son,
the one who **actually** merits
our justification before God the Father.

Consider also Galatians 6:14-15,
which says the following:

… far be it from me to boast
except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ,
by which the world has been crucified to me,
and I to the world.

15 For neither circumcision counts for anything,
nor uncircumcision, but a new creation.

End quote.

I believe that the “new creation” phrase in v. 15
is referencing the same reality of spiritual rebirth
which Ephesians 2:10 refers to as “created in Christ”.

Both the passage in 1 Corinthians and Galatians 6
are reflected in the statement from Galatians c. 5 v. 6
which says, “in Christ Jesus
neither circumcision nor uncircumcision
counts for anything,
but only faith working through love”.

Do you see the parallel ideas
present in these passages?

Galatians 5 and 6 in particular
treat circumcision status,
the outward works in this context, as irrelevant —
a zero sum in the analogical formula for salvation.

What matters
is described by the differing parallel descriptions
which all point to the same prerequisite.

Reduce these statements to algebraic formulas
for a moment.

If a+b=c, and a+b=c, then a+b=c.

If b = circumcised or not,
and c = salvation,
then what is a?

In this context,
a is the variable wording between these texts
which describes what is missing from our works.

It is the only thing remaining that truly matters
in the search for salvation,
since, as the text demonstrates,
neither our work nor our lack of work matters
since we already fall short
from our first instance of sin.

Ephesians 2 calls it “created in Christ”.
Galatians 6 calls it “a new creation”.
Galatians 5 calls it “faith working through love”.

What does Faith
working through godly love produce in us?

The regeneration of the Holy Spirit —
the new man —
the spiritual rebirth of our identity
by which we take **on** Christ to be found **in** Christ!

So, what is the explanation for a+b=c?

Our works amount to nothing,
so to arrive at the conclusion of salvation,
we must add to **our** zero sum merit
the **infinite** sum merit
of God’s grace through Faith.

Through Galatians,
we see that Scripture reinforces yet again
that we boast in Christ rather than ourselves,
and adds the final nail in the coffin of our works’ merit
by saying
that neither circumcision nor uncircumcision
counts for anything.

That is to say,
the presence of works doesn’t matter
in the context of meriting salvation,
because we are not under the law of works
but the law of Faith in which salvation comes
prior to and apart from any good works
that we may participate with.

All that said,
we must not minimize the importance of good works, because while they do not merit,
or purchase, salvation,
the Word found in the book of James
does not allow us to treat Faith as a barren claim.

So,
when God’s Word in James’s writing
says that Faith without works is dead,
He is not contradicting his Word in Paul’s writing
which says Faith is apart from works.

He is openly confronting counterfeit faith
which **claims** belief in Jesus
yet with no evidence to support
the assertion of true Faith.

In other words,
Paul’s explanation excludes works
as the meritorious basis of justification
while James’s explanation
exposes the reality of **barren** faith
that is **not truly** Faith at all.

Consider with me James c. 2 v. 14-26.

What good is it, my brothers,
if someone says he has faith but does not have works?
Can that faith save him?

15 If a brother or sister is poorly clothed
and lacking in daily food,
16 and one of you says to them,
“Go in peace, be warmed and filled,”
without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that?
17 So also faith by itself,
if it does not have works, is dead.

18 But someone will say,
“You have faith and I have works.”

Show me your faith apart from your works,
and I will show you my faith by my works.

19 You believe that God is one; you do well.
Even the demons believe—and shudder!

20 Do you want to be shown, you foolish person,
that faith apart from works is useless?

21 Was not Abraham our father justified by works
when he offered up his son Isaac on the altar?

22 You see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was completed by his works;
23 and the Scripture was fulfilled that says,
“Abraham believed God,
and it was counted to him as righteousness”—
and he was called a friend of God.

24 You see that a person is justified by works
and not by faith alone.
25 And in the same way
was not also Rahab the prostitute justified by works when she received the messengers
and sent them out by another way?

26 For as the body apart from the spirit is dead,
so also faith apart from works is dead.

End quote.

A common mistake made when reading this passage
is to ignore everything else
that Scripture says regarding Faith
and enforce single-minded focus
on the first 3 verses which suggest
that faith without works is no good, even dead.

However,
let’s first ask ourselves,
how might the context of this letter
inform our reading of this particular statement?

Consider this:
Why is it that the book of James
is considered by some to be
the “Proverbs of the New Testament”,
as is stated in the ESV’s introduction to this book?

That statement is reflective of the purpose of the book,
one quite similar to Proverbs:
that is to provide wisdom for daily living.

Now, consider this:
What is a critically important concept
to understand as it pertains to Faith for all of those who are called to the Great Commission,
that is, all Christians?

I’ll propose to you
that it is the need
for outwardly visible evidence of Faith
for the sake of those whom we would minister to.

If they see no fruit of Faith,
then all they see a dead faith.

This text in James is not one of those
primarily explaining how to acquire salvation.

All of **those** texts **clearly** preclude the ability to boast or teach meritorious works for justification before God.

The focus of this particular portion of Scripture is,
as it clearly states immediately
after referring to dead faith without works,
presenting us with a critical reason
that evident good works really matter:
showing evidence of our Faith —
shining the light of Christ in the world!

Nowhere in this text does James teach
that works can merit justification for salvation.

Rather,
he teaches that Faith which receives salvation
is living Faith
which does not remain barren
as the contradictory witness of counterfeit faith
which produces no good works
and may be described as dead.

Works complete Faith as its outward expression
rather than a component of its justification
for salvation.

In other words,
work is to Faith
as fruit is to tree —
completing the image of visible life of the plant.

This is how James can get away with
referring to Faith without works as dead,
because he’s making an analogy of Faith
by suggesting that Faith is not effective
for evangelism, ministry, or discipleship
if it isn’t demonstrated for people through good works.

Even prior to this section,
the first 13 verses of c. 2
are also more directly concerned
with the expressed representation of the Gospel
than they are with the mechanism of Faith.

Nutrients produce a tree’s fruit, not vice versa.

Likewise, a fruit of Faith,
such as good works, cannot produce Faith itself.

It is a natural consequence
rather than a causal factor.

Consider as another example the following analogy.

Good works are evidence for Faith
in the same way that an ID card
is evidence of citizenship.

You don’t have citizenship
**because** you have an ID card.

You have an ID card **because** you have citizenship, unless it’s a fake ID,
and there are probably an unfortunate number church-goers holding onto fake IDs as well
in the form of fake works —
facades to fool not just others,
but sometimes the ID holder themselves.

Those are the lukewarm, the pew-sitters,
or even the wolves in sheep’s clothing
in the case of those attempting to lead others astray from within our congregations,
whether they do so intentionally or not.

These are people who mimic
genuinely good works of the Spirit
through outwardly-appearing beneficial deeds,
and to many people this can pass as an ID
for proof of citizenship in the Kingdom of Heaven.

The problem
that such people must come to terms with eventually is that God judges the heart,
not merely the appearance of our deeds,
and only those who obtain genuine citizenship
in the Kingdom of God
will be allowed through the gates of eternity.

Your outwardly appearing good deeds
do not amount to genuinely good works
if they are a means of deception,
whether that’s to deceive others or yourself
into believing that you have genuine Faith.

Good works are not the root of Faith,
but the fruit of the root of Faith.

Returning to James c. 2,
I believe that the strongest case in favor
of human works meriting grace in this passage
is in v. 24
where it says that “a person is justified by works
and not by faith alone”.

However,
if you believe that this is referring
to justification before God for salvation,
you are reading that into the text
rather than reading it from the text,
because every other explicit statement
regarding **salvific** justification
clearly states that justification is not by works **at all**, or else it would not be according to grace.

I’ll say again,
these surface-level differences
cannot be true contradictions
because all Scripture is breathed out by God.

Therefore,
I must conclude that v. 24
is **not** suddenly changing the subject to explain
a contradictory means of justification for **grace**,
but continuing along with the **same** **purpose** of the rest of this passage:
that is to explain how we demonstrate our Faith
to others for the sake of evangelism and discipleship — how we justify our claim to Faith
in the eyes of **humanity**.

The text itself seems to make this argument
by reminding us in v. 23
that Abraham was counted righteous
for believing in God —
the same example
that is used by the same inspired Author
of all Scripture to explain in Romans c. 4
that Faith comes apart from works.

Set aside the narrow typical theological interpretation of the term “justify” for a moment
to consider with an open mind
the following paired claims.

We justify our Faith in Christ to people by our work.

God justifies the application of grace to us
by the received gift of Faith
on the basis of Christ’s work.

I believe that in the context of a text
that is inspired by an all-knowing and all-good God, that this is the only fair way to interpret
the passage in question,
and I know that every other discussion
pertaining to this topic that I’ve examined
that did not **exclusively** attempt to make a case
for works meriting **salvific** justification
has come to a similar conclusion
regarding the explanation
of this supposed pillar of the Roman Catholic doctrine of justification.

In fact,
I propose that when read fairly
in the intended context,
this text stands as yet another pillar
of the gifted nature of Faith,
by which we merit nothing
related to grace or salvation unto God.

Galatians 5:6, 1 Thessalonians 1:3,
and 2 Peter c. 1 v. 5-8 emphasize
that Faith works to produce fruit.

Consider the passage from 2 Peter:

… make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue, and virtue with knowledge,
6 and knowledge with self-control,
and self-control with steadfastness,
and steadfastness with godliness,
7 and godliness with brotherly affection,
and brotherly affection with love.

8 For if these qualities are yours and are increasing, they keep you from being ineffective or unfruitful
in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ.

What will be impacted for good
if we have these virtues active in our life?

Our ability to be **effective** and **fruitful**
with the knowledge of Christ.

That’s why there is no fruit in those
who have knowledge of Christ
yet without works to express the virtues of Christ.

As James explained,
we show our Faith to others through good works
which are enabled by the **first**
genuinely good work in our life —
the miracle of regeneration by the Holy Spirit.

By this,
we reflect the character of God,
not as the seller of salvation,
but as the cultivator of Christ-likeness!

## ***ACKNOWLEDGING REMAINING MYSTERY***

At this point,
I would like to acknowledge
the presence of remaining mystery
pertaining to the nature of God and the gift of Faith.

Throughout this episode,
we’ve traced the narrative and wisdom of Scripture
as it defines Faith and its implications
for our understanding of God
and our relationship with Him.

We can see that Faith is God’s gift —
freely given, genuinely received,
impossible apart from grace, not merited by our works, not imparted by proximity, and fruitful when genuine.

Yet, the exact inner mechanism by which divine grace, human response, foreknowledge,
and spiritual rebirth interact remains beyond us.

The degree of remaining mystery
will surely vary from person to person
as some of us may understand
more or less than others.

Thankfully,
we aren’t required to comprehend
the systematic theology or deeper mysteries of Faith
to begin taking part in it.

We are simply called to be obedient
in our reception of Faith
and let it produce the work of God in us.

In the absence of perfect understanding,
be encouraged by the Word from Romans c. 11 v. 33, which says:

Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom
and knowledge of God!
How unsearchable are his judgments
and how inscrutable his ways!

End quote.

Finally,
for those of you less concerned
about your own understanding
than you are another person’s salvation,
recall, in addition to God’s love
and desire to be known by his creation,
the following statement from Jesus
as recorded in John c. 3 v. 8.

8 The wind blows where it wishes,
and you hear its sound,
but you do not know where it comes from
or where it goes.
So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.

End quote.

You can appreciate the breeze
without knowing its coming and going,
but unlike a natural wind,
you can trust the wisdom of the Holy Spirit
to land on everyone who will genuinely receive Him.

The remaining mystery does not imply
that God is not trustworthy,
but that his ways are higher than our ways,
his thoughts are higher than our thoughts —
or one might say,
the wisdom of the Giver of Faith
is greater than that of the receiver of Faith.

## ***A PARABLE***

Prior to my final exhortation,
I’d like to present a parable that I wrote
in order to depict the biblical theology of Faith
as I understand it.

Like any parable,
the following is not meant to describe
every element of the related doctrines and theology,
but the relational depiction of gift, Giver, and receiver.

It’s not meant to represent
any specific individuals or churches,
but rather certain general responses to the Gospel.

The parable is as follows.

A benevolent King left his throne
to seek those of his people in need of help.

Beyond the gates of his palace,
he found four men starved to the brink of death.

The King offered each of the men a gift of food
and a room in his palace.

The first man refused the gifts,
so the King grieved the man’s fate
but left him with the consequences of his decision.

The second man pointed to the food
and began to arrogantly brag
as though he had earned this reward,
so the King left him outside the gate
where he continued to brag about the King’s offer
until he perished.

The third man accepted the King’s offer,
but told the others outside the gate
that he had no choice in the matter,
and so depicted the King as a tyrant
without regard for his subjects’ wills.

The fourth man humbly accepted the King’s offer
and thanked him for the food
and for enabling him to receive, by faith,
a secured future in the palace.

To explain the parable:
The first man refuses the gift.
His destruction is most obvious.

The second boasts as though he earned salvation,
but never genuinely accepted the gift,
so his destruction is assured as well.

The third received,
but misrepresented the King
as though grace disables a meaningful response.

The fourth received humbly,
thanked the King,
and knew even his faith rested upon the King’s mercy.

The call of Scripture is not to complicate faith,
to pull ourselves up by our bootstraps,
or to wait passively for an inevitable destiny.

It is to trust and obey Christ — 
to repent from sin, believe the Gospel,
and proclaim Jesus Christ as Lord:
the one true God and Messiah!

## ***FINAL EXHORTATION***

Finally,
to commence the true conclusion of this episode,
I am going to first provide a condensed form
of the theological conclusions
that I’m convinced are critical
for a proper theology of faith.

The following is adapted
from my personal statement of faith,
which I discussed at length in my prior episode,
the confession of faith,
with a slightly adjusted presentation
informed by the work I’ve done for this episode.

Because God is just
and all of mankind have fallen short
of the standard of righteousness,
we are dependent on God for salvation.

Yet, God does not force salvation upon us,
but by his will He requires that we respond
to repent and believe.

He judges each person perfectly
according to the degree of revelation received.

Spiritual rebirth produces good works in our lives,
but our good works cannot produce spiritual rebirth because no work of ours
can wash away the stain of sin.

Thus, salvation is acquired only by God’s grace through faith in Christ’s death and resurrection
by which He suffered the consequences of sin
for all believers
despite having never been guilty of sin Himself.

Furthermore,
even our faith is a gift from God,
and yet He still commands us to receive it.

By this,
God’s gift is shown to be not inconsiderately enforced
but lovingly offered.

Faith is a gift that originates from God's initiative
and is genuinely offered to all people
in conjunction with His foreknowledge
of who would receive it.

We are entirely dependent on His grace
for the opportunity and ability
to receive and act upon this gift.

At this time,
I believe that what I’ve presented in this episode
is an accurate description
of the doctrine of Faith
and its impact on biblical Christian theology
as I understand it.

Before I truly conclude this episode,
I want to acknowledge that people have tried
to systematize the mysterious mechanism of faith
in numerous different ways before now,
and many of them would disagree
on some of the finer details.

Some describe it as irresistible grace
with unconditional election.
Some others describe it as prevenient grace
with genuine response.

Yet others may not try to describe it
with any real detail,
but rather simply say what the Bible plainly says,
believe that whatever it means is true,
and do their best
to obey the simple command to believe and receive.

I just want to remind everyone
from every doctrinal camp
that hears this message
that whatever terms we use or reject,
we must preserve all that Scripture says:
faith is commanded,
faith is grace-enabled,
faith excludes boasting,
faith receives Christ,
and faith bears fruit.

At the end of the day,
what I think we need to remember
and communicate through our testimony is this:

Faith does not come through mere intellectual assent, emotional impulse,
religious adherence,
self-generated virtue,
or parroting doctrinal statements.

Faith comes through responding
to the invitation of God
unto holy spiritual rebirth.

It is the gift we are called,
but not coerced, to receive;
and your responsibility for your response to that gift
is not so that you can boast in your ability
to agree to a pardon you could never deserve,
but so that you can know
that your relationship with God is genuine.

He has proven his love for you
through the sacrifice of Jesus Christ,
and now He asks you to reciprocate love
through the one thing you can actually offer to God,
again, not through your own power
but through his enabling grace.

That offering is a repentant soul,
turned away from sin and surrendered to Him.

Even that, you owe to Him twice over —
first because He owns you by right as your Creator, and second, because He purchased your life
by the blood of Christ
from the just consequence of sin, which is death.

Jesus says in John c. 14 v. 6,

“I am the way, and the truth, and the life.
No one comes to the Father except through Me.”

By this we know that Jesus is **the** way.

There is no other way,
no side door into heaven,
no cheat code to eternity.

You are either saved through Faith in Jesus
or you are destined for Hell.
